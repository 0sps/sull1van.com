Sheesh, one full year. Today officially marks one year since I went sober.

## Background

I'd toyed with the idea of going sober ever since the middle of college. Everyone has a different relationship with substances, and mine with alcohol always felt like it should be temporary. As I got older and graduated, I figured that getting anything more than a light buzz should stay in my younger years. It felt like time to move on. But month after month I kept having nights where I got more drunk than intended, and I hated it. I could always control *whether* I drank -  I did plenty of fully sober stretches in college - but *when* I drank, I was never the one-or-two-and-stop kind of person. (the em dashes were me by the way, not ai) That's the thing I was trying to get past, and why a stretch of sobriety felt like a much-needed reset. I also just didn't like how I acted when I was drunk. I never did, to be honest.

What really kicked it off was some concerning blood tests in early 2025. They showed significant elevation in my ALT and AST, the key liver enzymes doctors use to detect liver stress and damage. The doctors mentioned I should probably slow down on drinking if that's what I'd been up to lately, since my liver was a little stressed. But reading the results, and seeing just how high the levels were, made me take my health a lot more seriously. I started [building a web dashboard](https://sull1van.com/fixing-blood-test-results) to track my blood test results so I could watch them closely, then put together [my own supplement stack](https://sull1van.com/best-liver-of-all-time) to help my liver.

At this point I still *wanted* to drink less, but weekend after weekend it never happened the way I hoped. I could never just have one drink and call it a night, and there were plenty of times I had way more than I expected. I won't even blame peer pressure - it was just me not knowing where my boundary sat. So one day that summer, I woke up and decided to cut it off entirely. Time to go sober.


## Rules of my sobriety

Sobriety felt pretty scary at first. I was more anxious than anything, constantly thinking stuff like *"What if I drink again? What if I mess up?"* For the first few days I was terrified I'd give in. I couldn't even picture a long-term goal yet, only that I had to make it two months. No idea what would happen after that. Just one day at a time.

As the weeks went by, I got less scared. Each day in those two months added another piece of evidence that I could absolutely live my life and not drink, and it started to feel more good than scary. Once I settled in, I started thinking about the whole thing more practically. At the start I didn't really know what counted as in-bounds versus out-of-bounds, so I reasoned that alcohol of ANY KIND must be banned. Mouthwash, even. But being sober is one of those things that sounds simple on the surface and turns out to be full of technicalities. What if I drink a "non-alcoholic" beer that actually has 0.1% or 0.5% alcohol? Technically I'd have to drink ~40 of them to equal one real beer - but it's still alcohol, right?

Like I said, at first I was strict about it. After a while, though, I realized the single drop of alcohol in an NA beer was never the problem I was trying to escape. I was literally just trying to stop getting drunk. So I made some new rules:
1. If it's labeled "NA" (0.5% alcohol or less), I could drink it.
2. If something was classified as alcoholic, meaning >0.5%, swallowing it counted as breaking sobriety.

That meant the trace alcohol in an NA beer was fine, and dipping my tongue to taste a friend's espresso martini was fine too, provided I didn't actually drink it. You might call these permissive rules, and that's fair. They worked for me. I was never anywhere near life-or-death alcoholism, so I didn't feel like my sobriety needed to be that strict as time went on. And since I'm writing this article, I can happily say I stuck to those rules for the ENTIRE year: I never once swallowed anything from an alcoholic beverage, was never drunk or buzzed in any way, and only ever drank NA beers, coffee, water, and soda. It feels both weird and pretty cool to say that.

---

My sobriety also ended up being threefold. Wanting it to feel more encompassing, more like a true personal reset, I folded in **smoking and vaping too, not just drinking**. I know plenty of people who don't do any of these things, so this doesn't make me special. I just wanted to make it a little more challenging and kill any chance of replacing one substance with another.


## Just go longer

Even though my sobriety was threefold, alcohol was the only part that ever made me afraid of relapse. Like I said, I told myself I was sober *for now*, not forever. I told my parents and friends I was doing two months, just to see where things went. I didn't commit to anything longer because I genuinely didn't think it was possible.

Cravings are like that. At first they seem impossible to shake, but then they fall off fast and don't really come back. As I neared the halfway point of those two months, the daily panic about when I'd drink next started to fade and things felt more normal. Around then, I caught up with a friend who gave me what might be the single best piece of advice I heard on this entire journey.

We got to chatting and I explained the sobriety, the two-month goal. He'd been sober his whole life, so it probably didn't seem like much of a challenge to him, and he asked why I'd settled on two months. I don't know, I told him. It was the best I thought I could do.

*"Why don't you just go longer?"*

I stared at him for a second. It had never even occurred to me that I could go longer than two months. But yeah, I could. Just go longer. So that's exactly what I did. At the end of the two months I decided to keep going. Two months turned into six, and at six I said twelve. Now here we are, twelve months later. It's crazy.


## How my body changed

The changes showed up fast, within about three months of going sober. I noticed more energy during the day and whiter eyes, honestly a great combo - I love how white my eyes are now. I also worked on my general health and designed a custom supplement stack I've been taking for about nine months. These biomarkers trended to the best levels I've ever tested for:
- ALT
- AST
- HDL Cholesterol
- LDL Cholesterol
- Fasted Glucose
- Hemoglobin A1c

This was a really big accomplishment for me. The people close to me know my liver enzymes were elevated, a sign of alcohol-induced "liver stress." It was never bad enough to be liver disease, but it was getting close. So it means a lot to say that, as of 2026, I've never had a better-functioning liver in my life. And that's saying something, since I've been getting these tests since I was seventeen!

Around the same time my liver improved and my eyes cleared up, the cravings evaporated. By month three I no longer had any big urge to drink. I settled into the reality that I'd just go to the bar with friends, sit my ass down, and order a soda or an NA beer. The lack of alcohol stopped feeling like a craving and started feeling like a routine: get the NA option, nothing else.

And on that note: NA beers are awesome. They're not for everyone, but a ton of stores and bars have started stocking them this past year, and it definitely beats a soda if you're out doing something social and want a drink in your hand. Even if you aren't a beer person, NA beers might make you one after you go sober, lol.


## What I miss

Here's something I already knew but see much more clearly now: I don't miss being drunk. What I miss is that nice buzz after one, maybe two drinks. But being *drunk*? Thinking back to college and how often I went out, I don't miss it at all. It used to be a lot of fun, but I know exactly what's waiting for me at the bottom of any bottle of something strong now, and it's a nasty hangover.

I actually saw that coming before I quit. In the weeks right before I went sober, I could feel the hangovers kicking in while I was still drunk. Heavy drinking had already started to lose its appeal, which is why I'd been slowly cutting down the number of nights a month I drank. I never want to go back to that - the seriously-drunk, four-plus-drink nights, the ones where you need an entire Sunday to recover, no matter how rare they'd become. Good riddance to those.

But that state where you have the tiniest buzz off a single drink? THAT is the one I miss, and that's honestly where my head is today. Not having alcohol is fine, I'll live, but I'd very much like to have it again in moderation someday. Without it, social commitments take on a slightly flatter feeling. Not meaningfully worse, just a little less excitement.

And the moments I actually miss are pretty specific and infrequent: you're at someone's house, out on a date, at a birthday dinner, and one drink would be perfect to soften the mood. There's also a regional layer to it. Drinking is just part of the culture here in the Midwest - we don't have as much of the great outdoors as other parts of the country, and we get some pretty brutal winters, so alcohol is baked into how people connect. Going 100% sober made that really obvious.

### Would I recommend others do this?

People ask me this all the time, and honestly? I probably wouldn't recommend it. There's something to be said for keeping alcohol as an option you can reach for now and then, the way you might have a sweet treat to celebrate a birthday. Or think of it like caffeine - you should be able to have a three-coffee day at work once in a while if that's what you want. I feel the same about alcohol: for people who can participate responsibly, I'd want them to take advantage of it where they can.

In an ideal world, that's how it would work for everyone. You'd have access to these substances, take whatever you wanted in moderation to feel the way you wanted to feel, and stop the moment you realized you were doing it too much and it was turning harmful. The catch is that last part. It takes a ton of introspection and honest self-assessment, so I don't think it actually plays out that way in real life. It'd be nice if it did.

That ideal matters precisely *because* of where it breaks down, and where it breaks down is addiction, not just alcoholism. People can get addicted to anything. One that a lot of people don't even think of is food addiction, where someone feels like they can't stop eating - and for those people, the moderation advice above just doesn't work. Picture walking up to an alcoholic in recovery and telling them, "you have to drink exactly two drinks a day, every single day for the rest of your life, no more, no less." He'd probably go insane. But that's food addiction. It's why I'm supportive of people using tools like GLP drugs to quiet the mental noise and subconscious cravings around food. I see a lot of shame and scoffing aimed at people who use these drugs for any reason other than diabetes, and I'd bet the people doing the scoffing have never dealt with an addiction of their own. It's easy to criticize how someone manages their addiction when you've never felt one yourself.

So that's the backdrop for my actual recommendation. I think sobriety is awesome, but I don't think everyone needs to do it. If you want to go permanently sober for *you*, then yes, go for it, and I'll be here cheering you on. But if you don't have a problem and you're just asking whether you *need* to go sober, I'd say you'll probably get more satisfaction out of holding yourself to one or two drinks a night, if you can. That's my take, at least.

On a related note, sobriety has also gotten me thinking about how many people abstain from all sorts of things in everyday life. If you're diabetic, you're probably not trying to eat loads of added sugar, whereas most people can have a couple slices of cake on a birthday just fine. If there are substances - alcohol included - that you'd like to consume less of, I'd honestly recommend doing some research on what could help. When I wanted to drink less, for instance, I experimented with naltrexone, which takes the high out of being drunk. It was nice to use at the time, but I don't like taking stuff I don't have to, so I don't have the prescription anymore.


## What I learned about people while sober

People ask constant questions, and most of them are supportive. I've fielded tons of questions about my sobriety and never once been shamed for it. What I do get sometimes is a quiet guilt - I'll see other people drinking and they almost seem upset that I'm not. It's like showing up to a friend's birthday with your own food because you're on a meal plan. It breaks a social norm and confuses people. The alternative is to stay home and skip social events, which is a terrible alternative.

The thing to actually watch out for is the opposite: the people who *encouraged* me to drink back when I was trying to cut down. I'd explained the goal to most of the people around me, and while the vast majority were supportive, a few would still ask me to get extremely drunk with them. And look, I'm NOT mad about it - that's exactly what I was known for up until 2025. But it's a weird feeling to push yourself in one direction after being known for the opposite, and to find the people around you aren't used to it or taking it seriously. You just have to stay vigilant, remember what your goals are and why you're chasing them, and stick to them no matter what anyone says.

I also have to add that there were _countless_ people who were incredibly supportive. People who'd start buying me an NA beer whenever I went out, and people who were just perfectly comfortable in those situations even when I wasn't drinking. That meant a lot, because it wasn't always the case.


## Will I stay sober past one year?

Probably not. For me this was about getting healthier and killing my craving to drink, and a year later I've gotten pretty much everything I was after. I want to keep this same relationship with alcohol going forward, just with the ability to have a drink if I want one.

It's also been incredible to see how much I could get done in the meantime, having repurposed all the weekends I wasn't going out. I refined my self-driving car project. I dialed in my health and got the best blood test results of my life. I worked on a passion project that graded two dozen phone chargers, picking up where a Google engineer left off. I built a half-dozen AI-assisted vibe coding projects, replacing things like my Spotify and my workout app with custom-coded alternatives that are 100% free to run (article coming soon). A lot of stuff.

I plan to return to drinking casually one day. Maybe not just yet, but one day. And if I still can't manage the one-or-two-a-night thing, I've got a year of sobriety proving I can always go 100% again if I need to.
