Sheesh, one full year. 
Today officially marks one year since I went sober.

## Background

I had toyed with the idea of going sober ever since the middle of college. Everyone has different relationships with substances, and mine with alcohol always felt like it should be temporary. As I got older and graduated, I felt like getting anything more than a light buzz should only ever be a part of my younger years. It felt like time to move on. But month after month, I kept having nights where I got more drunk than intended, and I hated it. Yes, I could always control whether I drank -  I did plenty of periods where I was entirely sober in college - but *when* I drank, I was never the kind of person who would have one or two and stop. (the em dashes were me by the way, not ai) That was the thing I was trying to get past, and why I felt like going sober for a while could be a much needed reset. I also just didn't like how I acted when I was drunk. I never did, to be honest.

What really kicked it off was some concerning blood tests I got in early 2025. The test showed significant elevation in my ALT and AST, key liver enzymes used to detect liver stress and damage. The doctors mentioned that I should probably slow down on drinking if that's what I had been up to lately, since my liver was a little stressed. But reading the results, and seeing just how high the levels were made me take my health much more seriously. I started doing things like [creating a web dashboard](https://sull1van.com/fixing-blood-test-results) to track my blood test results, so I could watch them closely. I then worked on [my own supplement stack](https://sull1van.com/best-liver-of-all-time) to help my liver.

At this point, I still did *want* to drink less, but weekend after weekend, it never happened the way I had hoped. I could never just have one drink and call it a night. There were many times where I had way more than I was expecting. I won't even say it was due to peer pressure, it was just me not knowing where my boundary sat. So one day in the summer, I woke up and decided to cut it off entirely. Time to go sober.


## Rules of my sobriety

Sobriety felt pretty scary at first. I was more anxious than anything, constantly thinking stuff like, *"What if I drink again? What if I mess up?"* 

For the first few days, I was terrified about giving in. I couldn't even imagine a long-term goal at this point, only that I had to make it two months sober. No idea what would happen after that. Just took it one day at a time.

As the weeks went by, I started to get less scared. And with each day in those two months, I started to collect evidence that I could absolutely live my life and not drink. 

It started to feel more good than scary. I also settled in to my sobriety and started to think about things more practically. Originally I didn't really know what was in the bounds vs. out of the bounds of being sober, so I reasoned that alcohol of ANY KIND must be banned. Mouthwash even. But as I became more confident in my ability to be responsible, I finally sorted out how I would live with this long-term. Being sober sounds really simple on the surface, but there are a lot of technicalities that can come up. For example, what if I drink a "non-alcoholic" beer that actually has 0.1% or 0.5% alcohol? Technically I'd have to have ~40 of these to equal one beer, but still, it's alcohol right?

Like I said, at first I was really strict with it. But after some time, I realized that the 1 drop of alcohol inside an NA beer was never the problem I was trying to escape. I was literally just trying to stop getting drunk. So I made some new rules for sobriety:
1. If it's labeled "NA" (0.5% alcohol or less), I could drink it.
2. If something was classified as alcoholic, so >0.5%, I counted it as breaking sobriety if I swallowed it. 

That meant the trace alcohol from an NA beer was fine, and dipping my tongue to taste a friend's espresso martini was also fine (provided I didn't actually drink it). You might say these are permissive rules, and that's fair. They worked for me. I was never in the territory of life-or-death alcoholism, so I didn't feel like my sobriety needed to be that strict as time went on. I'm also happy to say that, since I'm writing this article, I abided by those rules for the ENTIRE year. I never once swallowed anything from an alcoholic beverage, was never once drunk or buzzed in any way, and only ever drank NA beers, coffee, water, and soda. It feels both weird and pretty cool to say that.

---

Another point of my sobriety was that it actually became threefold. Realizing I wanted to make it more encompassing, and feel more like a true personal reset, I included **smoking and vaping as well, not just drinking**. 
I know plenty of people who don't do any of these things, so I don't think that makes me special in any way. I just wanted to make it a little more challenging and remove the idea that I might replace one substance with another.


## Just go longer

While my sobriety was threefold, it was only alcohol that made me afraid of relapse. As I said earlier, I told myself I was sober *for now*, not forever. I told my parents and friends I was doing it for two months, just to see where things went. I didn't commit to going longer because I genuinely did not think that was possible.

Cravings are like that. At first, they seem impossible to get rid of, but they quickly fall off and don't really come back. As I neared the halfway mark of those two months, the daily panic about when I would drink next started to fade, and things started to feel more normal. Around that time, I caught up with a friend who offered what might be the single greatest piece of advice I heard throughout this entire journey.

We got into chatting, and I explained the sobriety, my two-month goal. He had been sober his entire life, so this probably didn't seem too difficult for him. He asked why I settled on two months.

I don't know, I told him. It was the best I thought I could do.

*"Why don't you just go longer?"*

I stared at him for a second. It never even occurred to me that I could go longer than two months.

But yeah, I could. Just go longer. So that's exactly what I did. At the end of the two months I decided to keep going. Two months turned into six, then at six months I said twelve.  Now here we are, twelve months later. It's crazy.




## How my body changed

The changes in my body showed up fast, within ~3 months of going sober. I noticed increased energy during the day and whiter eyes. Honestly, a great combo. I love how white my eyes are now. I also worked on my general health and designed a custom supplement stack that I've been taking for about nine months now. These biomarkers trended to the best levels I've ever tested for:
- ALT
- AST
- HDL Cholesterol
- LDL Cholesterol
- Fasted Glucose
- Hemoglobin A1c

This was a really big accomplishment for me. The people close to me know my liver enzymes were elevated, indicative of alcohol-induced "liver stress". It was never bad enough to be liver disease, but it was getting close. I'm happy to say that, as of 2026, I've never had a better-functioning liver in my life. And that's saying a lot, since I have been getting these tests since I was seventeen!

Around the same time my liver began to improve and my eyes whitened, the cravings evaporated. By month three I no longer had any big urge to drink. I settled into the reality that I would just go to the bar with friends, sit my ass down, and order a soda or an NA beer. The lack of alcohol didn't really manifest as a craving anymore, more like it was just a routine I had to follow. Get the NA option, nothing else.

And on that note: NA beers are awesome. They're not for everyone, but a ton of stores and bars have started stocking them in this last year. It definitely beats a soda if you're doing something social and want a drink in your hand. Also, if you aren't a beer person, NA beers might make you one after you go sober, lol.




## What I miss

Something I already knew but see much more clearly now is that I don't miss being drunk. I do miss that nice buzz that comes after one, maybe two drinks. But being drunk? Thinking back to college and how often I would go out, I don't miss it. While it used to be a lot of fun, I know what's waiting for me now at the bottom of any bottle of something strong, and it's a nasty hangover.

I actually saw that coming before I quit. In the weeks right before I went sober, I could feel the hangovers kick in while I was still drunk. Drinking heavily had already started to lose its appeal, which is why I'd been slowly reducing the number of nights a month I drank. I don't ever want to go back to that state of getting seriously drunk, no matter how infrequent, the four-plus-drink kind of nights, the ones where you need an entire Sunday to recover. Good riddance to those.

But that nice state where you have the tiniest buzz from a single drink, THAT is the state I miss. And that's really where my head is today. Not having alcohol is fine, I'll live, but I would very much like to experience it again in moderation. Without alcohol, social commitments take on a slightly flatter feeling. Not significantly worse or anything, just a little less excitement. 

The moments of drinking that I miss are pretty specific and infrequent. You're at someone's house, out on a date, a birthday dinner, and one drink would be perfect to soften the mood. It's also just a part of culture here in the Midwest, we don't have as much of the great outdoors as other regions of the country, and we have some pretty bad winters. Alcohol is pretty ingrained into how people connect out here, and going 100% sober made that very obvious.

### Would I recommend others do this?
People have asked me this, and to them I probably wouldn't recommend it There's something to be said for having the option to use alcohol on occasion, to treat a drink the way you might have a sweet treat to celebrate a milestone like a birthday. Or even to treat it like caffeine: you should be able to have a three-coffee day once in a while at work if that's what you want. I feel the same way about alcohol. For those who can participate responsibly, I'd want them to take advantage where possible.

In an ideal world, that's how it would work for everyone: access to these substances, taking whatever they want in moderation to feel the way they want to feel, and stopping the moment they realized they were doing it too much and it was becoming harmful. But that last part takes a lot of introspection and careful self-assessment, so I don't think it actually happens that way. It would be nice if it did, though.

I think that ideal matters precisely because of where it breaks down, which is addiction, not just alcoholism. People can develop an addiction to anything. One that many people don't think of is food addiction, where someone feels like they can't stop eating. For those people, they can't follow the moderation advice above. Picture going up to an alcoholic in recovery and telling them, "you have to drink 2 drinks a day, every single day for the rest of your life, no more, no less." He'd probably go insane. But that's food addiction. And that's why I'm supportive of people using tools like GLP drugs to suppress mental noise and subconscious cravings for food. I see a lot of shame and scoffing aimed at people who use these drugs for any reason other than diabetes, and I feel like the people doing the scoffing are the ones who've never dealt with an addiction of any kind. It's easy to call out someone for how they manage their addiction if you've never felt that addiction yourself.

So that's the backdrop for my actual recommendation. While I think sobriety is awesome, I don't think everyone needs to do it. If you want to go permanently sober for *you*, then yes, go for it, and I'll be here to support you. But if you don't have a problem and you're just asking whether you *need* to go sober, I'd say you'll probably get more satisfaction out of limiting yourself to one or two drinks a night, if you can. That's my take, at least.

If you are diabetic, you probably won't be trying to eat loads of added sugar. But for most people, having mutiple slices of cake on birthdays would be fine for them. It was nice to use at the time, but I don't like taking stuff if I don't have to, do I don't have the prescription anymore.






## What I learned about people while sober

People ask constant questions, and most of them are supportive. I've had tons of questions about my sobriety and never once been shamed for it. What I do get, sometimes, is a quiet guilt. I'll see other people drinking and they almost seem upset that I'm not. Like if you went out for a friend's birthday and you brought your own food because you're on a meal plan. It breaks social norms and confuses people. The alternative is to stay home and skip social events, which is a terrible alternative.

The thing to actually watch out for is the opposite: people who *encouraged* me to drink back when I was trying to cut down. I'd explained the goal to most of the people around me when I wanted to drink less, and while the vast majority were supportive, some would still ask me to get extremely drunk together. And look, I am NOT mad about that — that's what I was known for up until 2025. But it is a weird feeling to push yourself in one direction after being known for the opposite and find the people around you aren't used to it or taking it seriously. You just have to be vigilant and remember what your goals are, and why you're pursuing them. And then stick to them, no matter what other people say.

I also have to add that there were _countless_ people who were incredibly supportive. People who'd start buying me an NA beer whenever I did go out. And people who were just perfectly comfortable in those situations with me even if I wasn't drinking. That meant a lot, because it wasn't always the case.



## Will I stay sober past one year?

Probably not. For me, it was about getting healthier and getting rid of my craving to drink. A year later, I've achieved pretty much everything I was looking for. I want to keep this same relationship with alcohol going forward, but with the ability to have a drink if I want one.

It's also been incredible to see how much I could accomplish in the in-between, having repurposed all the weekends I wasn't going out. I refined my self-driving car project. I honed my health and got the best blood test results of my entire life. I worked on a passion project that graded two dozen phone chargers, continuing a Google engineer's work. I created a half-dozen AI-assisted vibe coding projects, replacing stuff like my Spotify and my workout app with custom-coded alternatives that are 100% free to operate (article coming soon). A lot of stuff.

I plan to return to drinking casually one day. Maybe not just yet, but one day. And if I still can't do the 1-2 drinks a night thing, I've got a year's worth of sobriety showing me I can always go 100% again if I need to.
