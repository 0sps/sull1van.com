Today marks one full year since I went sober. Sheesh.


## Background

I had toyed with the idea of going sober ever since the middle of college. Everyone has different relationships with substances, and mine with alcohol always felt like it should be temporary. As I got older and graduated, it felt like time to move on, and let anything more than a light buzz stay back in college. But month after month, I kept having nights where I got more drunk than intended, and I hated it. I could always control whether I drank — I had plenty of totally sober stretches in college — but when I drank, I was never the guy who could have one or two and stop (The em dashes were me, not ai). That's what I hope would chave changed by now. I also just hated how I acted when I was drunk.

My path to sobriety didn't really take off until I got some concerning blood tests back in early 2025. The tests showed significant elevation in my ALT and AST, key liver enzymes used to detect liver stress and damage. My doctor mentioned that I should probably slow down on drinking if that's what I had been up to, but that he didn't think I had any kind of liver disease. But after seeing the results, and being more embarrassed than anything, I realized it was obvious what needed to change. It made me take my health much more seriously. I built [a web dashboard](https://sull1van.com/fixing-blood-test-results) to track my blood test results, so I could watch them closely, and then created [my own supplement stack](https://sull1van.com/best-liver-of-all-time) to help my liver.

These things did help, and they pushed me to really *want* moderation and to drink less. But weekend after weekend, I could never just have one drink and call it a night. I wouldn't even blame peer pressure, just that I never set a good boundary for myself. So one morning that summer, I woke up and decided to cut it off entirely. Time to go sober.


## Rules of my sobriety

Sobriety felt pretty scary at first. For the first few days I was terrified I'd give in, constantly thinking, *"What if I drink again? What if I mess up?"* I couldn't even picture a long-term goal, only that I had to make it for two months.

As the weeks went by, I started to get less scared. And with each day in those two months, I started to collect evidence that I could live my life and not drink. It started to feel more good than scary. 

Once I settled in, I started thinking about it more practically. At first I had no idea what was in bounds versus out, so I banned alcohol of ANY KIND. Mouthwash, even. But sobriety is less simple than it sounds, and there are a lot of technicalities. What about a "non-alcoholic" beer that actually has 0.1% or 0.5% alcohol? I would have to drink ~40 of those to equal one beer, but it's still alcohol, right? 

At first I was really strict about it, but after a while, I realized that a single drop of alcohol in the NA beer was never the thing I was trying to escape. I was just trying to stop getting drunk. So I made new rules:
1. If it's labeled "NA" (0.5% alcohol or less), I could drink it.
2. If something was classified as alcoholic, so >0.5%, it counted as breaking sobriety if I swallowed it.

That meant the trace alcohol from an NA beer was fine, and any kind of mouthwash or other alcohol was also fine if I rinsed out afterward. You might say these are permissive rules, and that's fair, they worked for me. I was never in the territory of life-or-death alcoholism, so I didn't feel like my sobriety needed to be that strict. And I stuck to these rules for the ENTIRE year, so it was really important that they at least be manageable ones.(It feels both weird and pretty cool to say that I did that)

---

Another point of my sobriety was that it actually became threefold. I wanted it to feel like a real personal reset, so I brought in **smoking and vaping too, not just drinking**. 
Plenty of people don't do any of these things, so I don't think I'm special in any way. I just wanted to make it a little more challenging and remove the chance I'd replace one substance with another.


## Just go longer

While my sobriety was threefold, it was only alcohol that made me afraid of relapse. Like I said, I told myself I was sober *for now*, not forever. I told my parents and friends I was doing it for two months, just to see where things went. I didn't commit to going longer yet until I knew I could.

Cravings are like that. At first, they seem impossible to get rid of, but they fall off fast and don't really come back. As I neared the halfway point of those two months, the daily panic about when I'd drink next started to fade and things felt more normal. Around that time, I caught up with a friend who gave me what might be the single best piece of advice I'd heard this entire year.

We got into chatting, and I explained the sobriety, my two-month goal. He had been sober his entire life, so this probably didn't seem too difficult for him. He asked why I settled on two months.

I don't know, I told him. It was the best I thought I could do.

*"Why don't you just go longer?"*

I stared at him for a second. It never even occurred to me that I could go longer than two months.

But yeah, I could. Just go longer. So that's exactly what I did. At the end of the two months I decided to keep going. Two months turned into six, then at six months I said twelve.  Now here we are, twelve months later. It's crazy.




## How my body changed

The changes in my body showed up fast, within ~3 months. I noticed increased energy during the day and whiter eyes, which is a great combo. I love how white my eyes are now. I also worked on my general health and refined that supplement stack I mentioned. Between the six-month to one-year period, these biomarkers trended to the best levels I've ever tested for:
- ALT
- AST
- HDL Cholesterol
- LDL Cholesterol
- Fasted Glucose
- Hemoglobin A1c

This was a really big accomplishment for me. The people close to me know my liver enzymes were elevated, not bad enough to be liver disease, but getting close. I'm happy to say that, as of 2026, I've never had a better-functioning liver in my life, and that's saying a lot since I have been getting these tests since I was **seventeen**!

Around the same time as all this, my cravings evaporated too. By month three I no longer had any big urge to drink. I settled into the reality that I would just go to the bar with friends, sit my ass down, and order a soda or an NA beer. The lack of alcohol didn't really manifest as a craving anymore, more like it was just a routine I had to follow. Get the NA option, nothing else.

## What I miss

Something I already knew but see much more clearly now is that I don't miss being drunk. I do miss that nice buzz after one, maybe two drinks. But thinking back to college times and how often I went out and got drunk for real, no, I don't miss it. It used to be a lot of fun, but I know what's waiting at the bottom of any bottle of something strong now, and it's a nasty hangover.

I actually saw that coming before I quit. In the weeks right before I went sober, I could feel the hangovers kicking in while I was still drunk. Heavy drinking had already lost its appeal, which is why I'd been slowly cutting the number of nights a month I drank. I don't ever want to go back to that, those nights where you need an entire Sunday to recover. Good riddance to those.

But that nice state where you have the tiniest buzz from a single drink? THAT is the state I miss. And that's really where my head is today. Not drinking is fine, I'll live, but I would like to experience it again in moderation. Without alcohol, social stuff takes on a slightly different feeling. Not worse, but just a little different.

The moments of drinking that I miss are pretty specific and infrequent. You're at someone's house, out on a date, at a birthday dinner, and one drink would be perfect to soften the mood. It's also just part of culture here in the Midwest. We don't have as much of the great outdoors as other regions, and we get some pretty bad winters, so a lot of times that is how we bond. Alcohol is ingrained into how people connect out here, and going 100% sober made that pretty obvious.

### Would I recommend others do this?
People have asked me this, and honestly I probably wouldn't recommend it There's something to be said for having the option to use alcohol on occasion, to treat a drink the way you might have a sweet treat to celebrate a milestone like a birthday. Or even to treat it like caffeine: you should be able to have a three-coffee day once in a while at work if that's what you want. I feel the same way about alcohol. For those who can participate responsibly, I'd want them to take advantage where possible.

In an ideal world, that's how it would work. You have access to these substances, take whatever you want in moderation to feel a specific way, and stop the moment you realized you’re doing it too much. But the last part takes a lot of introspection and honest self-assessment, so I don't think it actually plays out that way in real life. It'd be nice if it did.

To me that ideal matters because of where it breaks down, with addiction. People can truly get addicted to anything. One that a lot of people don't even think of is food addiction, where someone feels like they can't stop eating. And for those people, telling them to eat a specific number of calories just doesn’t work. Picture walking up to an alcoholic in recovery and telling them, "you have to drink exactly two drinks a day, every single day for the rest of your life, no more, no less." He'd probably go insane. But that's food addiction. It's why I'm supportive of people using tools like GLP drugs to quiet the mental noise and subconscious cravings around food. I see a lot of shame and scoffing aimed at people who use these drugs for any reason other than diabetes, and I wonder if it’s coming from people who have never dealt with an addiction of their own. 

So that's the background for my actual recommendation. I think sobriety is awesome, but I don't think everyone needs to do it. I think moderation is better. But if you, for any reason, aren’t someone who is blessed with the ability to consume it in moderation, and you want to go permanently sober for *you*, then yes, go for it! I'll be here cheering you on. But if you don't have a problem and you're just asking whether you *need* to go sober, I'd say you'll probably get more satisfaction out of holding yourself to one or two drinks a night, if you can. *That's my take, at least.*

## What I learned about people while sober

People ask constant questions, and most of them are supportive. I've had tons of questions about my sobriety and never once been shamed for it. What I do get, sometimes, is a quiet guilt. I'll see other people drinking and they almost seem upset that I'm not. Like if you went out for a friend's birthday and you brought your own food because you're on a meal plan. It breaks social norms and confuses people. The alternative is to stay home and skip social events, which is a terrible alternative.

The thing to actually watch out for is the opposite: people who *encouraged* me to drink back when I was trying to cut down. I'd explained the goal to most of the people around me when I wanted to drink less, and while the vast majority were supportive, some would still ask me to get extremely drunk together. And look, I am NOT mad about that — that's what I was known for up until 2025. But it is a weird feeling to push yourself in one direction after being known for the opposite and find the people around you aren't used to it or taking it seriously. You just have to be vigilant and remember what your goals are, and why you're pursuing them. And then stick to them, no matter what other people say.

I also have to add that there were _countless_ people who were incredibly supportive. People who'd start buying me an NA beer whenever I did go out. And people who were just perfectly comfortable in those situations with me even if I wasn't drinking. That meant a lot, because it wasn't always the case.



## Will I stay sober past one year?

Probably not. For me, it was about getting healthier and getting rid of my craving to drink. A year later, I've achieved pretty much everything I was looking for. I want to keep this same relationship with alcohol going forward, but with the ability to have a drink if I want one.

It's also been incredible to see how much I could accomplish in the in-between, having repurposed all the weekends I wasn't going out. I refined my self-driving car project. I honed my health and got the best blood test results of my entire life. I worked on a passion project that graded two dozen phone chargers, continuing a Google engineer's work. I created a half-dozen AI-assisted vibe coding projects, replacing stuff like my Spotify and my workout app with custom-coded alternatives that are 100% free to operate (article coming soon). A lot of stuff.

I plan to return to drinking casually one day. Maybe not just yet, but one day. And if I still can't do the 1-2 drinks a night thing, I've got a year's worth of sobriety showing me I can always go 100% again if I need to.
