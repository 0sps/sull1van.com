## Background
I had toyed with the idea of going sober ever since the middle of college. Everyone has different relationships with substances, and mine with alcohol always felt like it should be temporary — something I'd grow out of. As I got older and graduated, getting anything more than a light buzz started to feel like it belonged to my younger years. It felt like time to move on.

But month after month, I kept having nights where I got more drunk than I meant to, and I hated it. I could always control whether I drank — I did plenty of entirely sober stretches in college — but *when* I drank, I was never the kind of person who could have one or two and stop. (the em dashes were me by the way, not ai) That was the thing I was trying to get past. I didn't like who I was when I was drunk, either. I never had.

So I kept trying to cut down, and it kept not working. At one point I even got curious about the medication side of things. Working with my doctor, I tried naltrexone a couple of times just to see how it felt — it's a drug people use to take the edge off cravings and drink less. It was fine, honestly. It really does take the high out of getting drunk. I didn't get much use out of it, though, so I only tried it those couple of times. Weekend after weekend, I still couldn't just have one drink and call it a night.

What really kicked things into gear was some concerning blood tests I got in early 2025. The test showed significant elevation in my ALT and AST, key liver enzymes used to detect liver stress and damage. The doctors told me I should probably slow down on drinking if that's what I'd been up to lately, since my liver was a little stressed. But it was reading the results myself — seeing just how high the numbers were — that made me take my health seriously. I built [a web dashboard](https://sull1van.com/fixing-blood-test-results) to track my blood test results so I could watch them closely, and I put together [my own supplement stack](https://sull1van.com/best-liver-of-all-time) to help my liver.

The blood test wasn't the origin of any of this. It was the accelerant. I still *wanted* to drink less, and it still wasn't happening the way I'd hoped. There were many nights I had way more than I planned. I won't even blame peer pressure — it was just me not knowing where my boundary sat. So one morning that summer, I woke up and decided to cut it off entirely. Time to go sober.

## Rules of my sobriety
Sobriety felt scary at first. Mostly I was anxious, constantly thinking, *"What if I drink again? What if I mess up?"* For the first few days I was terrified I'd give in. I couldn't picture a long-term goal — only that I had to make it two months. No idea what came after that. Just one day at a time.

As the weeks went by, I got less scared. Each day, I collected a little more evidence that I could live my whole life and not drink. It started to feel more good than scary.

Once I settled in, I started thinking about it practically. At first I didn't really know what was in bounds versus out, so I decided alcohol of ANY KIND was banned. Mouthwash, even. But being sober sounds simpler on the surface than it is — there are a lot of technicalities. What about a "non-alcoholic" beer that actually has 0.1% or 0.5% alcohol? Technically I'd have to drink ~40 of these to equal one beer. But it's still alcohol, right?

At first I was really strict. After a while, though, I realized the single drop of alcohol in an NA beer was never the thing I was trying to escape. I was just trying to stop getting drunk. So I made new rules:

1. If it's labeled "NA" (0.5% alcohol or less), I could drink it.
2. If something was classified as alcoholic (>0.5%), it counted as breaking sobriety if I swallowed it.

That meant the trace alcohol in an NA beer was fine, and dipping my tongue to taste a friend's espresso martini was fine too (as long as I didn't actually drink it). You might call these permissive rules. That's fair. They worked for me. I was never in the territory of life-or-death alcoholism, so my sobriety didn't need to be that strict. And since I'm writing this article, I can say I abided by those rules for the ENTIRE year. I never once swallowed anything from an alcoholic beverage, was never drunk or buzzed in any way, and only ever drank NA beers, coffee, water, and soda. It feels both weird and pretty cool to say that.

---

My sobriety also became threefold. I wanted it to feel like a true personal reset, so I folded in **smoking and vaping as well, not just drinking**. I know plenty of people who don't do any of these things, so this doesn't make me special. I just wanted to make it a little harder, and to remove any chance of trading one substance for another.

## Just go longer
While my sobriety was threefold, only alcohol made me afraid of relapse. I told myself I was sober *for now*, not forever. I told my parents and friends I was doing it for two months, just to see where things went. I didn't commit to longer because I genuinely didn't think longer was possible.

Cravings are like that. At first they seem impossible to shake, but they fall off fast and don't really come back. As I neared the halfway point of those two months, the daily panic about when I'd drink next started to fade, and things felt more normal. Around then, I caught up with a friend who gave me what might be the single best piece of advice of this entire journey.

We got to chatting, and I explained the sobriety, the two-month goal. He'd been sober his whole life, so this probably didn't seem hard to him. He asked why I settled on two months.

I don't know, I told him. It was the best I thought I could do.

*"Why don't you just go longer?"*

I stared at him for a second. It had never even occurred to me that I could go longer than two months.

But yeah. I could. Just go longer. So that's what I did. At the end of the two months I decided to keep going. Two months turned into six, then at six months I said twelve. Now here we are, twelve months later. It's crazy.

## How my body changed
The changes showed up fast, within ~3 months. I noticed more energy during the day and whiter eyes — a great combo. I love how white my eyes are now. I also kept refining that custom supplement stack, which I've been on for about nine months. These biomarkers trended to the best levels I've ever tested for:

- ALT
- AST
- HDL Cholesterol
- LDL Cholesterol
- Fasted Glucose
- Hemoglobin A1c

The people close to me know my liver enzymes were elevated, indicative of alcohol-induced "liver stress." It was never bad enough to be liver disease, but it was getting close. As of 2026, I've never had a better-functioning liver in my life. That's saying a lot, since I've been getting these tests since I was seventeen.

Around the same time my liver improved and my eyes whitened, the cravings evaporated. By month three I had no big urge to drink. I settled into just going to the bar with friends, sitting my ass down, and ordering a soda or an NA beer. The lack of alcohol stopped feeling like a craving and started feeling like a routine. Get the NA option, nothing else.

And on that note: NA beers are awesome. They're not for everyone, but a ton of stores and bars have started stocking them this past year. It beats a soda if you want a drink in your hand at something social. And if you're not a beer person, going sober might turn you into one, lol.

## What I miss
Something I already knew but see much more clearly now: I don't miss being drunk. I do miss that nice buzz after one, maybe two drinks. But being drunk? Thinking back to college and how often I went out, I don't miss it. It used to be a lot of fun, but I know what's waiting at the bottom of any bottle of something strong now, and it's a nasty hangover.

I actually saw that coming before I quit. In the weeks right before I went sober, I could feel the hangovers kick in while I was still drunk. Heavy drinking had already started to lose its appeal, which is why I'd been slowly cutting the number of nights a month I drank. I don't ever want to go back to that — the four-plus-drink nights, the ones where you need an entire Sunday to recover. Good riddance.

But that state where you have the tiniest buzz off a single drink — THAT is what I miss. And that's really where my head is today. Not drinking is fine, I'll live, but I'd very much like to experience it again in moderation. Without alcohol, social commitments take on a slightly flatter feeling. Not worse, exactly. Just a little less excitement.

The moments I miss are specific and infrequent. You're at someone's house, out on a date, at a birthday dinner, and one drink would be perfect to soften the mood. It's also just part of the culture here in the Midwest. We don't have as much of the great outdoors as other regions, and we get some pretty bad winters. Alcohol is ingrained into how people connect out here, and going 100% sober made that very obvious.

### Would I recommend others do this?
People ask me this, and I probably wouldn't recommend it. There's something to be said for having the option to use alcohol on occasion — to treat a drink the way you might have a sweet treat to celebrate a birthday. Or to treat it like caffeine: you should be able to have a three-coffee day at work once in a while if that's what you want. I feel the same way about alcohol. For those who can participate responsibly, I'd want them to take advantage where they can.

In an ideal world, that's how it works: you have access to these substances, take what you want in moderation to feel a specific way, and stop the moment you notice you're doing it too much. But that last part takes a lot of introspection and honest self-assessment, and in real life it often doesn't play out that way. It'd be nice if it did.

That ideal matters to me because of where it breaks down — with addiction. And I say "addiction" on purpose, because I spent a long time not saying it about myself. People can get addicted to anything, food included, where telling someone to simply eat a specific number of calories just doesn't work. Picture walking up to an alcoholic in recovery and saying, "you have to drink exactly two drinks a day, every day for the rest of your life, no more, no less." He'd go insane. That's what "just eat less" sounds like to someone fighting that fight.

It's why I'm supportive of people using tools like GLP drugs to quiet the mental noise and the subconscious cravings. I see a lot of shame and scoffing aimed at people who use these drugs for any reason other than diabetes, and I wonder if that scoffing comes from people who've never dealt with an addiction of their own. I get the appeal firsthand, because I tried naltrexone myself — just curiosity, really, a couple of times with my doctor to see how it felt. I didn't end up needing it. But I was willing to reach for something that might help, and I'd never scoff at anyone else for doing the same.

So that's the background for my actual recommendation. I think sobriety is awesome, but I don't think everyone needs to do it. Moderation is better. But if you, for whatever reason, aren't blessed with the ability to consume it in moderation, and you want to go permanently sober for *you*, then yes — go for it. I'll be here cheering you on. And if you don't have a problem and you're just asking whether you *need* to go sober, I'd say you'll probably get more out of holding yourself to one or two drinks a night, if you can. *That's my take, at least.*

## What I learned about people while sober
People ask constant questions, and most of them are supportive. I've had tons of questions about my sobriety and never once been shamed for it. What I do get, sometimes, is a quiet guilt. I'll see other people drinking and they almost seem upset that I'm not — like you'd brought your own food to a friend's birthday because you're on a meal plan. It breaks a social norm and confuses people, even when nobody means anything by it. It's not shame aimed at you; it's a small discomfort that you've made visible just by opting out. The only alternative is to stay home and skip social events, which is a much worse one.

The thing to actually watch out for is the opposite: people who *encouraged* me to drink back when I was trying to cut down. I'd explained the goal to most people around me, and while the vast majority were supportive, some would still ask me to get extremely drunk together. And look, I am NOT mad about that — that's what I was known for up until 2025. But it's a weird feeling to push yourself in one direction after being known for the opposite, and find that the people around you aren't used to it or aren't taking it seriously. You just have to stay vigilant, remember your goals and why you're chasing them, and stick to them no matter what anyone says.

I also have to add: there were _countless_ people who were incredibly supportive. People who'd start buying me an NA beer whenever I went out. People who were perfectly comfortable in those situations with me even when I wasn't drinking. That meant a lot, because it wasn't always the case.

## Will I stay sober past one year?
Probably not. For me, this was about getting healthier and getting rid of my craving to drink. A year later, I've achieved just about everything I was after. I want to keep this same relationship with alcohol going forward, but with the ability to have a drink if I want one.

It's also been incredible to see how much I could do with the weekends I wasn't going out. I refined my self-driving car project, honed my health to the best blood test results of my life, graded two dozen phone chargers to continue a Google engineer's work, and built a half-dozen AI-assisted vibe coding projects — custom, free-to-run replacements for things like my Spotify and workout apps (article coming soon).

I plan to return to drinking casually one day. Maybe not just yet, but one day. And if I still can't do the one-or-two-drinks-a-night thing, I've got a year of sobriety showing me I can always go 100% again if I need to.

That's the part that matters most: a year ago I couldn't get a single drink to stay a single drink. Now I have proof I can stop entirely, whenever I decide to. Sober or not, that's the freedom I was actually after.
