Today marks one full year since I went sober. Sheesh.

## Background

I'd toyed with the idea of going sober ever since the middle of college. Everyone has a different relationship with substances, and mine with alcohol always felt like it should be temporary. The heavy weekend drinking was something I figured I'd grow out of. As I got older, anything past a light buzz mostly just gave me headaches, and it stopped being fun. It felt like time to move on.

But the further I got from college, the more it kept not happening. Month after month I'd get more drunk than I meant to, and I hated it. I could always control *whether* I drank — I had plenty of totally sober stretches in college — but *when* I drank, I was never the guy who could have one or two and stop. (The em dashes are me, by the way, not AI.) That was the thing I was trying to get past. I also just hated how I acted when I was drunk. Always have, honestly.

Things kicked into gear in early 2025, when I got some concerning blood tests back. They showed significant elevation in my ALT and AST, key enzymes for detecting liver stress and damage. My doctor said I should probably drink less if that's what I'd been up to, but that he wasn't too worried. Reading the results myself is what actually pushed me. I built [a web dashboard](https://sull1van.com/fixing-blood-test-results) to track my results so I could watch them closely, and I put together [my own supplement stack](https://sull1van.com/best-liver-of-all-time) to help my liver.

The blood test didn't start any of this. It just sped it up. I still *wanted* to drink less and it still wasn't happening — I won't even blame peer pressure, I just didn't know where my boundary sat. So one morning that summer I woke up and decided to cut it off entirely. Time to go sober.

## Rules of my sobriety

Sobriety felt pretty scary at first. For the first few days I was terrified I'd give in, constantly thinking, *"What if I drink again? What if I mess up?"* I couldn't even picture a long-term goal, only that I had to make it two months. No idea what came after. Just took it one day at a time.

As the weeks went by I got less scared, and each day I collected a little more evidence that I could live my whole life and not drink. It started to feel more good than scary.

Once I settled in, I started thinking about it more practically. At first I had no idea what was in bounds versus out, so I banned alcohol of ANY KIND. Mouthwash, even. But sobriety is less simple than it sounds, and there are a lot of technicalities. What about a "non-alcoholic" beer that actually has 0.1% or 0.5% alcohol? I'd have to drink ~40 of those to equal one real beer, but it's still alcohol, right? At first I was really strict about it. After a while though, I realized that single drop of alcohol in an NA beer was never the thing I was trying to escape. I was just trying to stop getting drunk. So I made new rules:

1. If it's labeled "NA" (0.5% alcohol or less), I could drink it.
2. If something was classified as alcoholic (>0.5%), it counted as breaking sobriety if I swallowed it.

That meant the trace alcohol in an NA beer was fine, and dipping my tongue to taste a friend's espresso martini was fine too, as long as I didn't actually drink it. You might call these permissive rules, and that's fair. They worked for me. I was never in the territory of life-or-death alcoholism, so my sobriety didn't need to be that strict. And I stuck to those rules the **ENTIRE year**: never swallowed anything from an alcoholic drink, never once drunk or buzzed, only ever NA beers, coffee, water, and soda. It feels both weird and pretty cool to say that.

---

My sobriety also ended up being threefold. I wanted it to feel like a real personal reset, so I folded in **smoking and vaping too, not just drinking**. Plenty of people don't do any of these things, so I don't think it makes me special. I just wanted to make it a little harder and remove any chance of trading one substance for another.

## Just go longer

Even though my sobriety was threefold, only alcohol ever made me afraid of relapse. Like I said, I told myself I was sober *for now*, not forever. I told my parents and friends two months, just to see where it went. I didn't commit to longer because I genuinely didn't think longer was possible.

Cravings are like that. At first they seem impossible to shake, but they fall off fast and don't really come back. As I neared the halfway point, the daily panic about when I'd drink next started to fade and things felt more normal. Around then I caught up with a friend who gave me what might be the single best piece of advice of this whole thing.

We got to chatting and I explained the sobriety, the two-month goal. He'd been sober his whole life, so this probably didn't seem hard to him, and he asked why I settled on two months. I don't know, I told him. It was the best I thought I could do.

*"Why don't you just go longer?"*

I stared at him for a second. It had honestly never occurred to me that I could go longer than two months. But yeah, I could. Just go longer. So that's what I did. At the end of the two months I kept going. Two months turned into six, then at six I said twelve. Now here we are, twelve months later. It's crazy.

## How my body changed

The changes showed up fast, within ~3 months. I noticed more daytime energy and whiter eyes, which is a great combo. I love how white my eyes are now. I also kept refining that custom supplement stack, which I've been on about nine months. These biomarkers trended to the best levels I've ever tested for:

- ALT
- AST
- HDL Cholesterol
- LDL Cholesterol
- Fasted Glucose
- Hemoglobin A1c

The people close to me know my liver enzymes were elevated, indicative of alcohol-induced "liver stress." It was never bad enough to be liver disease, but it was getting close. As of 2026, I've never had a better-functioning liver in my life, and that's saying a lot since I've been getting these tests since I was **seventeen**.

Around the same time, the cravings evaporated. By month three I had no big urge to drink. I settled into going to the bar with friends, sitting my ass down, and ordering a soda or an NA beer. The lack of alcohol stopped feeling like a craving and started feeling like a routine. Get the NA option, nothing else.

And on that note: **NA beers are awesome.** They're not for everyone, but a ton of stores and bars have started stocking them this past year. Beats a soda if you want a drink in your hand at something social. And if you're not a beer person, going sober might turn you into one, lol.

## What I miss

Something I already knew but see much more clearly now is that I don't miss being drunk. I do miss that nice buzz after one, maybe two drinks. But being drunk? Thinking back to college and how often I went out, no, I don't miss it. It used to be a lot of fun, but I know what's waiting at the bottom of any bottle of something strong now, and it's a nasty hangover.

I actually saw that coming before I quit. In the weeks right before I went sober, I could feel the hangovers kicking in while I was still drunk. Heavy drinking had already lost its appeal, which is why I'd been slowly cutting the number of nights a month I drank. I never want to go back to that, no matter how rarely — the four-plus-drink nights, the ones where you need an entire Sunday to recover. Good riddance to those.

But that state where you've got the tiniest buzz off a single drink, THAT is what I miss. And that's really where my head is today. Not drinking is fine, I'll live, but I'd genuinely like to experience it again in moderation. Without alcohol, social stuff takes on a slightly flatter feeling. Not worse, exactly, just a little less exciting.

The moments I miss are specific and pretty rare. You're at someone's house, out on a date, at a birthday dinner, and one drink would be perfect to soften the mood. It's also just part of the culture here in the Midwest. We don't have as much of the great outdoors as other regions, and we get some pretty bad winters. Alcohol is ingrained into how people connect out here, and going 100% sober made that really obvious.

### Would I recommend others do this?

People ask me this, and honestly I probably wouldn't recommend it. There's something to be said for having the option to drink on occasion, treating a drink like a sweet treat on a birthday. Or like caffeine — you should be able to have a three-coffee day at work once in a while if that's what you want. I feel the same about alcohol. For people who can participate responsibly, take advantage where you can.

In an ideal world that's exactly how it works: you take what you want in moderation to feel a specific way, and you stop the moment you notice you're doing it too much. But that last part takes a lot of introspection and honest self-assessment, so I don't think it actually plays out that way in real life. It'd be nice if it did.

That ideal matters to me because of where it breaks down, which is addiction. And I say "addiction" on purpose, because I spent a long time not saying it about myself. People can get addicted to anything, food included, and telling someone to just eat a specific number of calories doesn't work. Picture walking up to an alcoholic in recovery and telling them, "you have to drink exactly two drinks a day, every single day for the rest of your life, no more, no less." He'd go insane. That's what "just eat less" sounds like to someone in that fight.

It's why I'm supportive of people using tools like GLP drugs to quiet the mental noise and subconscious cravings around food. I see a lot of shame and scoffing aimed at people who use these drugs for any reason other than diabetes, and I wonder if that's coming from people who've never dealt with an addiction of their own. I'd never scoff at anyone for reaching for something that might help.

So here's my actual recommendation. I think sobriety is awesome, but I don't think everyone needs to do it. Moderation is better. But if, for whatever reason, you're not someone who's blessed with the ability to drink in moderation, and you want to go permanently sober for *you*, then yes, go for it! I'll be here cheering you on. And if you don't have a problem and you're just asking whether you *need* to go sober, you'll probably get more out of holding yourself to one or two drinks a night, if you can. *That's my take, at least.*

## What I learned about people while sober

People ask constant questions, and almost all of them are supportive. I've had tons of questions about my sobriety and never once been shamed for it. What I do get, sometimes, is a quiet guilt. I'll see other people drinking and they almost seem upset that I'm not, kind of like if you brought your own food to a friend's birthday because you're on a meal plan. It breaks a social norm and confuses people, even when nobody means anything by it. The alternative is to stay home and skip social events, which is much worse.

The thing to actually watch out for is the opposite: people who *encouraged* me to drink back when I was trying to cut down. I'd explained the goal to most people around me, and while the vast majority were supportive, some would still ask me to get hammered together. And look, I am **NOT** mad about that, because that's what I was known for up until 2025. But it's a weird feeling to push yourself in one direction after being known for the opposite and find that the people around you aren't used to it or taking it seriously. You just have to stay vigilant, remember your goals and why you're chasing them, and stick to them no matter what anyone says.

I also have to add that there were _countless_ people who were incredibly supportive. People who'd start buying me an NA beer whenever I went out. People who were perfectly comfortable in those situations with me even when I wasn't drinking. That meant a lot, because it wasn't always the case.

## Will I stay sober past one year?

Probably not. For me this was about getting healthier and killing my craving to drink, and a year later I've gotten pretty much everything I was after. I want to keep this same relationship with alcohol going forward, but with the ability to have a drink if I want one.

It's also been incredible to see how much I got done with all the weekends I wasn't going out. I refined my self-driving car project. I honed my health and got the best blood test results of my life. I worked on a passion project that graded two dozen phone chargers, continuing a Google engineer's work. And I built a half-dozen AI-assisted vibe coding projects, custom free-to-run replacements for things like my Spotify and workout apps (article coming soon). A lot of stuff.

I plan to return to drinking casually one day. Maybe not just yet, but one day. And if I still can't do the one-or-two-drinks-a-night thing, I've got a year of sobriety showing me I can always go 100% again if I need to. A year ago I couldn't get a single drink to stay a single drink, and now I know I can stop entirely whenever I decide to. That's the part I'm actually happy about.
