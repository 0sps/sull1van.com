One full year. 
Today officially marks one full year since I went sober.

## Background

I had toyed with the idea of going sober basically ever since I graduated college. It felt weird to be constantly drinking the way I did in school when I had a real job and real adult responsibilities to deal with.

Everyone has different relationships with substances, and mine with alcohol always felt like it should be temporary. I felt like getting anything more than a light buzz should only ever be apart of my yougner years. I drank plenty in high school and college, so when I graduated, it felt like time to move on. But as the months went by, I kept having night after night where I got more drunk than inteneded, and I hated it. Yes, I could always control whether I drank -  I did plenty of months where I was entirely sober in college - but I was never the kind of person who would have one or two drinks and stop. (those em dashes were ME btw, not ai) That was the thing I was trying to get past, and why I felt like going sober for a while could be a much-needed reset.

What really kicked it off, though, was some concerning blood tests I got in early 2025. They made me take my health much more seriously. I started doing things like [creating a web dashboard](https://sull1van.com/fixing-blood-test-results) to track my blood test results, so I could watch them closely as they hopefully improved. 

I wanted to drink less, both for my mind and for my liver. But weekend after weekend, that never happened the way I had hoped. So one day I woke up and decided to cut it off entirely. Time to go sober.


## The rules of my sobriety

It felt pretty daunting at first. I was more scared than anything, constantly asking things like "What if I drink again? What if I mess up?" For the first few days, I was terrified about giving in. There was no long-term goal, only just to make it two months sober. No idea what would happen after that, whether I would relapse or go longer. I just took it one day at a time.

As the weeks went by, I started to get less scared. With each day, I started to collect evidence that I could live my life and *not* drink. 

It felt good. I also started to think about things practically. Originally I didn't really know what was in the bounds vs. out of the bounds of being sober, so I finally sorted that out. While being sober sounds easy on the surface, there are a lot of technicalities that can come up. For example, what if I drink a "non-alcoholic" beer that actually has 0.1% or 0.5% alcohol? 

At first I was REALLY strict with it. But after some time, I realized that the 1 drop of alcohol inside an NA beer was never the problem I was trying to escape. I was literally just trying to stop getting drunk. So I made some new rules for my sobriety:
1. If it's labeled "NA" (0.5% alcohol or less), I could drink it.
2. If something truly had alcohol, I counted it as breaking sobriety if I swallowed it. 

That meant the trace alcohol from an NA beer was fine, and dipping my tongue to taste a friend's espresso martini was also fine (provided I didn't actually drink it).

You might say these are permissive rules, and that's fair. They worked for me. I was never in the territory of life-or-death alcoholism, so I didn't feel like my sobriety needed to be that strict. I'm also happy to say that, since I'm writing this article, I abided by those rules for the ENTIRE year. I never once swallowed anything from an alcoholic beverage, was never once drunk or buzzed in any way, and only ever drank NA beers, coffee, water, and soda. It feels both weird and pretty cool to say that.

---

Another point of my sobriety was that it was actually threefold. It included **smoking and vaping, not just drinking**. I know plenty of people who don't do any of these things, so I don't think that makes me special in any way, but I just wanted to make it a little more challenging. Also, I've never been huge on weed or nicotine, so I thought they were good to add in. 


## Just go longer

While my sobriety was threefold, it was only alcohol that made me afraid of relapse. As I said earlier, I told myself I was sober for now, not forever. I told my parents and friends I was doing it for two months, just to see where things went. I didn't commit to going longer because I genuinely did not think that was possible.

Cravings are like that. At first, they seem impossible to get rid of, but they quickly fall off and don't really come back. As I neared the halfway mark of those two months, the daily panic about when I would drink next started to fade, and things started to feel more normal. Around that time, I caught up with a friend who offered what might be the single greatest piece of advice I heard throughout this entire journey.

We got into chatting, and I explained the sobriety, my two-month goal. He had been sober his entire life, and so he asked why I settled on two months.

I don't know, I said. It was the best I thought I could do.

He heard that and said: *"Why don't you just go longer?"*

I stared at him for a second. It never even occurred to me that I could go longer than two months.

But yeah, I could. Just go longer. So that's exactly what I did. Two months turned into six, then at six months I said twelve. Here we are, twelve months later. It's crazy.




## How my body changed

The changes in my body showed up fast, within ~3 months. I noticed increased energy during the day and whiter eyes. Honestly, a great combo. I love how white my eyes are now. I also worked on my general health and designed a custom supplement stack that I've been taking for about nine months now. These biomarkers trended to the best levels I've ever tested for:
- ALT
- AST
- HDL Cholesterol
- LDL Cholesterol
- Fasted Glucose
- Hemoglobin A1c

This was a really big accomplishment for me. The people close to me know my liver enzymes were elevated, indicative of alcohol-induced "liver stress". It was never bad enough to be liver disease, but it was getting close. I'm happy to say that, as of 2026, I've never had a better-functioning liver in my life. And that's saying a lot, since I have been getting these tests since I was seventeen!

Around the same time my liver began to improve and my eyes whitened, the cravings evaporated. By month three I no longer had any big urge to drink. I settled into the reality that I would just go to the bar with friends, sit my ass down, and order a soda or an NA beer. The lack of alcohol didn't really manifest as a craving anymore, more like it was just a routine I had to follow. Get the NA option, onthing else.

And on that note: NA beers are awesome. They're not for everyone, but a ton of stores and bars have started stocking them in this last year. It definitely beats a soda if you're doing something social and want a drink in your hand. Also, if you aren't a beer person, NA beers might make you one after you go sober, lol.




## What I miss

Something I already knew but see much more clearly now is that I don't miss being drunk. I do miss that nice buzz that comes after one, maybe two drinks. But being drunk? Thinking back to college and how often I would go out, I don't miss it. While it used to be a lot of fun, I know what's waiting for me now at the bottom of any bottle of something strong, and it's a nasty hangover.

Near the start of my sobriety (the weeks before), I could feel the hangovers kick in while I was still drunk. Drinking heavily had already started to lose its appeal, which is why I had been slowly reducing the number of nights a month I drank. I don't ever want to go back to that state of getting seriously drunk, no matter how infrequent. The four-plus-drink kind of nights, the ones where you need an entire Sunday to recover. Good riddance to those.

But that nice state where you have the tiniest buzz from a single drink, THAT is the state I miss. And that's really the center of this whole year. Not having alcohol is fine, I'll live, but I would very much like to experience it again in moderation. Without alcohol, social commitments take on a slightly flatter feeling. Not significantly worse or anything, just a little less excitement. 

The moments of drinking that I miss are pretty specific and infrequent. You're at someone's house, out on a date, a birthday dinner, and one drink would be perfect to soften the mood. It's also just a part of culture here in the Midwest, we don't have as much of the great outdoors as other regions of the country, and we have some pretty bad winters. Alcohol is pretty ingrained into how people connect out here, and going 100% sober made that very obvious.

**My recommendation for you**
And that's why I wouldn't recommend going fully sober to most people. There's something to be said about having the option to use alcohol as a tool. To treat a drink the same way you might want a sweet treat to celebrate a milestone or a birthday. Or maybe even to treat it like caffeine: you should have the ability to have a three-coffee day once in a while at work, if that's what you want. For those who have the opportunity to participate in that responsibly, I would want you to take advantage where possible. A lot of people have asked me this question, and while I think sobriety is awesome, I don't think everyone needs to do it. If you want to go permanently sober for *you*, then yes, go for it! I'll be here to support you. But if you don't have a problem, and you're asking me if you need to go sober, I would say you might get more satisfaction out of limiting yourself to one or two drinks a night, if you can. That's my take at least.




## What I learned about people while sober

People ask constant questions, and most of them are supportive. I've had tons of questions about my sobriety and never once been shamed for it. What I do get, sometimes, is a quiet guilt. I'll see other people drinking and they almost seem upset that I'm not. Like if you went out for a friend's birthday and you brought your own food because you're on a meal plan. It breaks social norms and confuses people. The alternative is to stay home and skip social events, which is a terrible alternative.


The thing to actually watch out for is the opposite: people who *encouraged* me to drink back when I was trying to cut down. I'd explained the goal to most of the people around me when I wanted to drink less, and while the vast majority were supportive, some would still ask me to get extremely drunk together. And look, I am NOT mad about that — that's what I was known for up until 2025. But it is a weird feeling to push yourself in one direction after being known for the opposite and find the people around you aren't used to it or taking it seriously. You just have to be vigilant and remember what your goals are, and why you're pursuing them. And then stick to them, no matter what other people say.

I also have to add that there were _countless_ people who were incredibly supportive. People who'd start buying me an NA beer whenever I did go out. And people who were just perfectly comfortable in those situations with me even if I wasn't drinking. That meant a lot, because it wasn't always the case.



## Will I stay sober past one year?

Probably not. For me, it was about getting healthier and getting rid of my craving to drink. A year later, I've achieved pretty much everything I was looking for. I want to keep this same relationship with alcohol going forward, but with the ability to have a drink if I want one.

It's also been incredible to see how much I could accomplish in the in-between, having repurposed all the weekends I wasn't going out. I refined my self-driving car project. I honed my health and got the best blood test results of my entire life. I worked on a passion project that graded two dozen phone chargers, continuing a Google engineer's work. I created a half-dozen AI-assisted vibe coding projects, replacing stuff like my Spotify and my workout app with custom-coded alternatives that are 100% free to operate (article coming soon). A lot of stuff.

I plan to return to drinking casually one day. Maybe not just yet, but one day. And if I still can't do the 1-2 drinks a night thing, I've got a years worth of sobriety showing me I can always go 100% again if I need to.
