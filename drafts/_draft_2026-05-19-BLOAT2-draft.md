I have been weirdly excited to write this one.

Earlier this year, I had some bloodwork come back with liver enzymes that were much higher than I wanted. Not emergency-room bad, not "you need to panic" bad, but definitely high enough to make me stop treating this like an abstract health thing.

For context, I have been getting blood tests since I was seventeen. That gives me something most people do not have: a real personal baseline. Not just a normal range on a lab report, but actual younger-me data that I could compare against.

So the goal became very simple.

I did not want a normal liver. I wanted the best liver of all time.

Or at least, the best liver test results I have ever personally recorded. B.L.O.A.T.

## The baseline

The main markers I cared about were pulled from a Comprehensive Metabolic Panel. People call this a "liver panel" casually, but most of these values are part of a broader metabolic panel. I filtered it down to the ones that seemed most relevant:

- Total protein
- Albumin
- Globulin
- Albumin/globulin ratio
- Total bilirubin
- Alkaline phosphatase
- AST
- ALT

ALT and AST were the ones I cared about most because they are the classic liver stress enzymes. They are not perfect. They do not tell the entire story. But if you have been drinking and those numbers are high, they are absolutely going to get your attention.

Albumin, total protein, globulin, and A/G ratio are more about the broader picture. They say something about protein status, immune activity, and how well the body is maintaining basic chemistry. Bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase are in there too, but bilirubin can move around for reasons that are not automatically scary, and alkaline phosphatase is not purely liver-specific.

In other words, this was never going to be a single-number game. I needed the whole picture to look good.

Here was seventeen-year-old me:

| Analyte | 2018 report, age 17 |
| --- | ---: |
| Total protein | 7.1 g/dL |
| Albumin | 4.7 g/dL |
| Globulin | 2.4 g/dL |
| A/G ratio | 2.0 |
| Total bilirubin | 0.7 mg/dL |
| Alkaline phosphatase | 90 U/L |
| AST | 28 U/L |
| ALT | 17 U/L |

Pretty solid. Annoyingly solid, honestly.

Then there was twenty-four-year-old me:

| Analyte | 2025 report, age 24 |
| --- | ---: |
| Total protein | 6.7 g/dL |
| Albumin | 4.4 g/dL |
| Globulin | 2.3 g/dL |
| A/G ratio | 1.9 |
| Total bilirubin | 0.7 mg/dL |
| Alkaline phosphatase | 54 U/L |
| AST | 147 U/L |
| ALT | 71 U/L |

Yeah. Not my favorite table.

AST at 147 and ALT at 71 are not "whoops, tiny fluctuation" numbers. Something was clearly going on. Given my life at the time, alcohol was the obvious first suspect.

## Step one: stop drinking

The first move was easy to understand and hard to actually do: stop drinking.

There was no world where I was going to fix my liver while continuing to feed it the exact thing that was probably annoying it. I had already been thinking about going sober for a while, but the bloodwork made it feel much less optional. It went from "maybe this would be a good personal reset" to "okay, your body is now giving you actual spreadsheet feedback."

So I stopped.

I expected the numbers to magically snap back right away. They did improve, but not as dramatically or as instantly as I hoped. That was when this turned from a simple sobriety project into a full health project.

I started looking into what else might support liver recovery, especially around oxidative stress and glutathione.

## The glutathione rabbit hole

I had been around glutathione before. Back in 2022, a men's clinic told me my liver looked stressed and put me on intramuscular glutathione. It was probably 400 mg weekly, if I remember right.

The problem was that injecting it hurt like hell.

My liver was mostly normal at the time, so it did not feel worth it. I stopped and basically forgot about it for a few years.

When I came back to the topic in 2025, I had the same basic question: can I just take this stuff orally and avoid stabbing myself?

The annoying answer is: kind of, maybe, it depends.

Plain glutathione has a bioavailability problem. One newer paper on glutathione analogues says native oral glutathione has bioavailability below 1% because it gets broken down enzymatically and does not absorb well through the gut. That same paper tested modified versions of glutathione and found that one N-methylated analogue had much better half-life and oral bioavailability than native glutathione in preclinical testing. Interesting, but also not exactly something I can buy at Target.

The more practical thing I found was S-acetyl glutathione, usually shortened to SAG.

SAG is basically a modified glutathione molecule. The pitch is that the acetyl group makes it more stable, helps it get into cells, and then the body can convert it back into glutathione. That is the theory, at least.

The best human data I found was a small 2018 crossover study in eighteen healthy volunteers. It compared a single large oral dose of S-acetyl glutathione against regular glutathione. The study found higher plasma GSH Cmax and AUC after SAG than after the regular glutathione product, although the erythrocyte results were messier and actually favored regular glutathione on some measures. So, not a slam dunk. But it was enough to make SAG look more plausible than random supplement marketing.

There was also a 2025 safety assessment in *Food and Chemical Toxicology* that looked at SAG for use in foods and dietary supplements. The summary I found reported no genotoxic signal, an acute oral LD50 above 2000 mg/kg in rats, and a 13-week rat NOAEL of 1500 mg/kg/day. That does not prove anything magical about efficacy, but it did make the safety profile look less sketchy than I expected.

At that point, my conclusion was pretty simple: oral glutathione itself is probably not the move, but S-acetyl glutathione is at least biologically interesting enough to try.

## The stack

I ended up building a supplement stack around glutathione support and general inflammation reduction. This is what I settled on:

- S-acetyl-L-glutathione, 200 mg per day
- NAC, 600 mg per day
- TMG, 4 g per day
- Turmeric, 1 g per day
- Grape seed extract
- Fish oil, specifically Nordic Naturals Complete Omega Xtra
- Elderberry extract, 500 mg per day

The core of the liver idea was SAG plus NAC. NAC is a precursor for glutathione synthesis, and SAG was my attempt at getting something closer to glutathione into the body without injections. TMG was there mostly because I was interested in methylation and metabolic health more broadly. Fish oil and turmeric were there for general inflammation. Elderberry was not really a liver thing. I just like having it in the stack.

Was this perfectly controlled science? Absolutely not.

This was one person changing several things at once: no alcohol, better diet, supplements, more health tracking, and a lot more motivation than usual. So I am not going to sit here and pretend I know exactly which lever did what.

But after running the stack, the numbers came back into the normal range.

Nice.

Unfortunately, "normal" was not the goal. The goal was B.L.O.A.T.

## Going one step further

After the oral stack got me back into a safer place, I wanted to see if I could beat my old baseline outright.

So I brought back injectable glutathione.

This time I used 600 mg weekly. Still painful, still annoying, still not something I would casually recommend. I treated it like a short-term intervention, not a new lifestyle identity.

This is also the part where I should be very clear: I am not telling anyone to inject glutathione. Injections have sterility risks, dosing risks, and sourcing risks. Do not freestyle medical stuff just because some guy with a blog got excited about his lab report.

For me, though, the logic was simple. I had used it before. I had tolerated it before. I wanted to run the experiment long enough to see whether my bloodwork could beat seventeen-year-old Sean.

Then May 2026 came around.

## The rematch

Here is the oldest test versus the newest test:

| Analyte | 2018 report, age 17 | 2026 report, age 25 | My read |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Total protein | 7.1 g/dL | 7.2 g/dL | Basically identical, with 2026 slightly higher. |
| Albumin | 4.7 g/dL | 4.7 g/dL | Dead even. I will absolutely take that. |
| Globulin | 2.4 g/dL | 2.5 g/dL | Stable. Not better, not worse in any meaningful way. |
| A/G ratio | 2.0 | 1.9 | Slightly lower, but still good. |
| Total bilirubin | 0.7 mg/dL | 1.0 mg/dL | Higher, but not something I care much about in isolation. |
| Alkaline phosphatase | 90 U/L | 79 U/L | Better in 2026. |
| AST | 28 U/L | 25 U/L | Better in 2026. |
| ALT | 17 U/L | 18 U/L | Functionally tied. Both are excellent. |

This is the point where I started laughing at the lab report.

Because yeah, I think 2026 wins.

Not by a mile. It is not like every single marker is better. Albumin tied, ALT was one point higher, A/G ratio was slightly lower, and bilirubin rose a bit. If I were trying to lawyer the table against myself, there are places to do it.

But the liver enzymes, the ones that started this whole thing, are ridiculous now. AST dropped from 147 to 25. ALT dropped from 71 to 18. Even compared to my age seventeen baseline, AST is better and ALT is basically the same.

That is insane to me.

## What I think actually worked

If I had to rank the interventions, I would put sobriety at the top by a lot.

No supplement is going to outsmart alcohol if alcohol is the main thing stressing your liver. That was the boring answer from the start, and it was probably the correct one.

After that, I do think the glutathione work may have helped. The research is not perfect, but it is at least coherent. Glutathione matters. Plain oral glutathione has absorption problems. Modified versions like SAG are interesting because they try to solve that problem. NAC is a known precursor. The injectable route bypasses the gut entirely.

That all makes sense on paper.

But I still want to be careful here. My bloodwork does not prove that SAG worked. It does not prove NAC worked. It does not prove injections worked. It proves that the entire intervention, taken together, lined up with the best liver numbers I have ever had.

That is good enough for a blog post. It is not good enough for a clinical claim.

## The verdict

I started with a pretty simple goal: get my liver back into range.

Then, because I cannot leave anything alone, I turned it into a competition against my own teenage bloodwork.

And somehow, I won.

As of May 2026, I have the best liver test results of my life. Maybe not every single marker, if you want to be extremely annoying about it. But as a full picture, especially with AST and ALT, this is the best my liver has ever looked on paper.

B.L.O.A.T. achieved.
